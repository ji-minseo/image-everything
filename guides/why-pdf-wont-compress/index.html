Why Your PDF Won’t Get Smaller (and What Actually Works)

By Image Everything Editorial · Published October 18, 2026

Learn why text, scans, fonts and already-compressed images respond differently to PDF optimization.

Try the tool: Compress a PDF ↗

A PDF is a container, not an image

A PDF may contain selectable text, vector drawings, embedded fonts, photographs, form fields, attachments and metadata. These parts compress differently. A document made mainly of text and vector elements can already be compact; there may be little to shrink without changing its content.

By contrast, a scanned document may contain a full-page photo for every page. Image-heavy PDFs often have more room for savings, depending on how those images were encoded.

Why compression sometimes saves almost nothing

The images may already be highly compressed. They may use encodings the chosen optimizer cannot safely rewrite. The file may be mostly text, so changing JPEG quality has no effect. Signatures, attachments or other structure can also limit safe transformations.

An optimizer that preserves original text and vectors will not necessarily meet a fixed target size. A target is a goal, not a guarantee.

Use image-aware controls thoughtfully

Try a moderate JPEG quality adjustment first. The impact depends on whether compatible JPEG images are embedded in the PDF. If the resulting file is not smaller, some tools deliberately retain the original rather than replace it with a larger export.

For image-heavy scans, reducing scanning resolution or grayscale conversion in the original scanning workflow may be more effective than recompressing the finished PDF. This requires access to the source or rescanning.

Avoid destructive shortcuts

Rasterizing every page to an image may discard selectable text, alter searchable content and harm sharp vector graphics. Printing to PDF can change form behavior, links, bookmarks or document accessibility. Editing or resaving a signed PDF can invalidate signatures.

When the document matters, verify links, forms, signatures and selectable text after any optimization.

When you must meet an attachment threshold

First check whether the receiver accepts a cloud link instead of an attachment. If not, remove unnecessary high-resolution photos from the source document, export again, and inspect the result. Splitting a PDF can work only if the receiver accepts multiple files.

A 10 MB target checkbox cannot override the underlying content without tradeoffs. Be wary of any tool that promises exact sizes with zero quality change.

How our PDF workflow behaves

Our browser PDF compressor attempts structural and supported JPEG-image optimization while preserving text and vector pages rather than rasterizing them. The optional target-size setting reports whether the goal was reached; it does not force extra quality loss beyond the selected setting. Always keep the original and review the download.

A diagnosis based on document origin

Ask how the document was created. An office export with selectable text and vector charts is a different compression problem from photographs of paper saved as PDF pages. If you can still edit the source, resizing oversized photos before re-exporting often gives you better control than repeatedly rewriting the PDF.

Our PDF attachment guide walks through a practical file limit. If a PDF contains images that need separate preparation, start with the image compression workflow before generating a new document.

Assess what you must preserve

For a simple flyer, visual sharpness may be the deciding factor. For a form, functioning fields, signatures, selectable text and accessibility may matter more than bytes. Compression is not always appropriate for a signed or official PDF. Keep untouched originals and compare behavior in a PDF reader.

A size target cannot guarantee a smaller result without accepting changes to the content. When a tool honestly reports no savings, that is useful diagnostic feedback rather than necessarily a failure.

Consider a five-page contract created from a word processor versus a five-page document made from full-resolution photographs of paper. Although the page counts match, the latter can be dramatically larger because every photographed page contains pixel data. This comparison explains the mechanism but is not a measured claim about any particular document.

For a PDF attachment limited to 10 MB, begin by recording its current size and whether it contains mainly text or embedded scans. Try a quality adjustment on a duplicate, measure the output, and check text selection, links, forms and signatures. If the target is not reached, changing the source images may be the only reasonable path.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my PDF bigger after compression?

Rewriting can add overhead or use less efficient encodings. A safe workflow should compare results and retain the smaller original when appropriate.

Does selecting Under 10 MB guarantee a 10 MB result?

No. It is a best-effort goal; document contents and chosen quality can limit reductions.

Can compression invalidate a digital signature?

Yes. Modifying the file may affect signatures and document integrity. Verify signed documents carefully.

Why didn't lowering quality change my text-only PDF?

JPEG image quality affects compatible embedded images, not text objects or vector drawings.

Measured samples: optimization can grow a PDF

Using PyMuPDF 1.26.7, two generated single-page PDFs were saved in both a basic and an optimized form. The text/vector sample measured 54,789 bytes normally and 63,450 bytes with garbage collection and deflate enabled; the optimized version was larger. A separate image-bearing page measured 4,803,518 bytes normally and 197,565 bytes optimized. No pages or image dimensions were deliberately changed. Results depend strongly on file internals and export settings; these are controlled library tests rather than results from Image Everything's PDF Compressor.
